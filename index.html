Free-air haptics for video and games

We are rebuilding Disney Research's AIREAL device for less money, improving on it, and connecting it to both video playback and a video game. It fires rings of air that you can feel on your skin from up to a metre away, with nothing to wear or hold.

Quinn · Nush · Theresa · Hasan

Project overview

The need

Video games and videos mostly give you sound and picture. The only touch feedback is usually vibration, and that needs something against your body, like a controller or wearable gear.

Problem statement

Design a device that can induce various tactile sensations on a user's body, for gaming and video entertainment purposes, without specialized wearable equipment.

Where we're starting from: AIREAL (SIGGRAPH 2013)

AIREAL pushes air out of a sealed box with five small subwoofers. The air leaves through a flexible nozzle and forms a stable vortex ring. A pan/tilt gimbal aims the nozzle, and a depth camera tracks the user so the rings hit where they should.

What we're changing

  • CheaperOff-the-shelf parts and hobby 3D printing instead of research-lab hardware, under a $600 budget.
  • WirelessThe device talks to the computer without a cable.
  • Video playbackHaptic effects synced to what's happening on screen.
  • Video gameReal-time effects triggered by in-game events.

Objectives, constraints and criteria

Objectives

  • A noticeable sensation up to 1 m away.
  • Touch feedback that isn't vibration or contact-based.
  • No heavy assembly or complicated setup.
  • Wireless link to the computer.
  • Total cost under $600.

Constraints

  • Nothing worn; adapts to different poses with no sensors on the body.
  • Sensation arrives no more than 500 ms after the audio/video.
  • Maximum power draw of 100 W.
  • Takes real-time input from another device.
  • Effects adapt to the content being shown.

Criteria

  • Range of the sensation (m)
  • Latency (ms)
  • Power use (W)
  • Cost ($)

Main tasks

The work splits into three areas. These are the planned work packages, not final design decisions.

Mechanical

Air, nozzle and aiming

  • Vortex chamber: a sealed enclosure with the speaker mounts
  • Nozzle and aperture design and testing (AIREAL's best was about 2 cm)
  • Pan/tilt gimbal that can cover a 75° or wider field
  • Parts designed for low-cost FDM 3D printing
  • Housing and mounting so setup is simple

Electrical

Drive, control and power

  • Choose the speakers or actuators
  • Amplifiers and the waveform drive circuit
  • Microcontroller with a wireless link
  • Servo or motor drive for the gimbal
  • Power supply and budget within 100 W; wiring or PCB

Software

Tracking, aiming and content

  • Track the user without anything worn (camera-based)
  • Calibrate the camera to the device and convert targets into pan/tilt angles
  • Pulse timing and wireless protocol
  • Sync with video playback
  • Game integration and keeping latency under 500 ms end to end
